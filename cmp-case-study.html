What to Build, and What Not to Build

Ben Siverly · Part 1 of 3

Vehicle Service Request Management

I read these four ticket themes as one core problem: there isn't a single source of truth that everyone can see and trust, so people have devised their own workarounds and systems that lead to duplicate tickets and degraded day-to-day operations.

To address this, I'd build the record first, make it reach people second, and hold shop coordination until both exist.

The same problem can be found wearing different clothing in finance. When you eat out and add a tip, the settled charge doesn't match the pending authorization from the night before, but there's enough overlap to match them algorithmically. In the commercial fleet world, the specific vehicle, issue flagged, driver, and date give us enough information to pattern-match tickets and force them into a standardized format with an enforced lifecycle. (note: I would specifically look into using VMRS (Vehicle Maintenance Reporting Standards), owned and maintained by ATA's Technology & Maintenance Council (TMC), the de facto industry taxonomy)

Once issues are de-duplicated properly, the other three concerns feel much more manageable.

  • Notifications can reliably fire for issues, since you know you aren't spamming someone three different ways about what is essentially the same core issue.
  • Severity and priority can be addressed to deliver safety flags specific to commercial fleet maintenance so the top issue to attend to is always the highest risk to safety and revenue impact.
  • Shop coordination - on the surface this is the weightiest concern because it's the core platform we're offering, but the coordination we can deliver with de-duplicated issue tracking as the foundation ships in half the time.

What people experience

A driver experiences a disruption while driving and calls dispatch to log it. He relies on a safe, well-maintained vehicle to do his job, so lack of clarity on status is an anxiety catalyst for him. When he doesn't hear back from dispatch, he goes in and adds the issue himself. He then tells his supervisor; and for her the brake issue is a compliance mandate, so she also goes in and logs it. Three tickets from three angles on the same truck's brakes, leading to four phone calls and zero proactive updates.

That's likely where the 98 complaints about duplicates in 90 days come from, and those tickets do not mark the brake problem as different from a cosmetic scratch reported earlier that same week, so the office keeps the real truth on a whiteboard (like the mid-size operator on Call 2). The driver, still waiting on the app, calls dispatch for an update on when he should bring the truck in... When the repair completes, he has to call dispatch again to find out or wait for his supervisor to do the same.

Everything these companies pay for sits downstream of a record everyone can see and trust.

What it costs

A single untriaged brake report ages in a flat queue until it becomes a roadside breakdown that costs three to five times planned cost (benchmark) and puts the safety of drivers at risk. Downtime around $450 to $760 per vehicle-day (benchmark), penalty exposure on the missed delivery, potential workplace litigation if the 'deferred emergency' has an audit trail one could point to. Industry benchmarks put a single unplanned breakdown at $3,000 to $9,000 all-in.

And that's just one event. The tickets undercount how many there are: a ticket is a complaint about a pattern, not a count of events. Fleets average an unscheduled event every 10,000 to 33,000 miles (benchmark), which makes service events a daily reality across 1,800 customers (extrapolated), and the information given tells us a meaningful share arrive through more than one channel.

And the human on the front lines: let's call it 15 status calls per dispatcher per day at four minutes each, an hour of every dispatcher's shift answering a question the system should make obvious.

The bigger cost is the whiteboard itself. If the wall holds the truth, the system of record has already left the platform, and the renewal is priced on being the operating system. Every workflow that moves to the wall also moves out of reach of the agent layer built on this same backend. They hired us to drive their operations, not to input sticky notes.

What to build first

The system cannot work as expected until you've got one record per real-world issue, enforced at the only layer that can enforce it. Reports arrive through the app, supervisors, phone calls, agents, and soon telematics. No interface can enforce uniqueness across channels; a real-world issue has no natural global ID. So we need to construct one the way financial systems dedupe transactions: fingerprint matching on aggregate fields. Start with vehicle, defect category, and time window, confidence-scored. High-confidence matches attach to the existing request automatically; borderline ones get suggested to the dispatcher. Either scenario is reversible.

The first shippable piece is just the matcher, and it can run on top of what already exists. Point it at the current ticket table, let it flag probable duplicates, and have dispatchers confirm or reject the suggestions.

That covers the first few weeks. The rest of this build is the enforced lifecycle. Reported, triaged, scheduled, in service, ready, closed. Transitions become the only way state changes, and every transition can emit an event so consumers subscribe instead of polling. The status field stops being a suggestion and starts being a fact.

The outcome is that the record lands in the industry's standard shape: defects and repairs coded to VMRS, the maintenance taxonomy fleets and shops already share. That shape makes the rest of the product easier to manage.

What to build second

Once the record is trustworthy, it has to reach people. Today, finding out what's happening means opening the app and hunting for the right ticket. Consumer software settled this years ago: the thing you're waiting on can keep you posted. Call 3 gave us the spec: every update should answer two questions, can I keep driving, and when will it be fixed. Those two answers are the required payload of every notification. The person who reported an issue should hear back automatically, and anyone else can follow a ticket to receive the changes without going looking. Even better if the way they hear back isn't an email. SMS and voice are the right defaults. No new app, no new login - channel preference gets validated during dispatcher shadowing.

Severity sets the volume, and by then it's close to free: VMRS coding derives priority from the defect itself rather than a dropdown someone guesses at, with out-of-service criteria defining safety-critical. Routine transitions might go through text but safety-critical reports call the point person to ensure things are addressed. Only a human can downgrade or close them. The full agent-versus-human boundary is in the Part 2 rationale.

Why not shop coordination?

I'd call this a sequencing decision, not a rejection. Eighty-four tickets, second by volume, stated by the largest customers... but building coordination now would export today's incoherent state to the shops; garbage in motion at higher velocity is still garbage. Once the record exists, this is a webhook consumer plus a scoped status-write endpoint for shops, on rails that already exist.

Their pain gets solved faster by not being first.

What would change my mind

  • Duplicate rate doesn't fall → the match keys are not working; fix matching before adding anything.
  • Auto-merge precision slips → drop to suggest-only merging and use the data to improve the auto-merge mechanism. Automation is a privilege the system has to earn.
  • Status calls persist despite visible state → the channel is wrong, not the state; shift entirely to a push.
  • Downgrade rate runs high → threshold for severity is miscalibrated; retune before dispatchers start disregarding our flags.

One marker outranks the dashboards: if customers are still relying on a whiteboard after 90 days either the product didn't earn trust or inertia is stronger than we thought, and I'd go find out which.

What this signal doesn't tell us

I read this the way I'd read any dataset: ticket counts are complaint data, not volume data. Only the frustrated file them, and generally when they're pretty frustrated. The calls are three accounts rather than a survey, and I'm not sure they map to the customer base overall or have intrinsic skew. I'd want:

  • The distribution of incoming reports (product analytics, plus shadowing two dispatchers): This sets the dedupe match keys.
  • Number of status calls per dispatcher per day (and minutes each!) tells us the ROI baseline. I'd also get call logs from CS to verify.
  • I want a photo of the board. This is the cheapest and likely most telling discovery artifact available, likely needs me to work with their account manager.
  • Share of requests that are safety-critical, by defect category. I'd love to see a minimum of twelve months of request data to help set classifier targets and alert budget.
  • The share of customers running FMCSA-regulated vehicles: Mandated DVIR workflows come with a set structure, and our CS team would know who fits the mold.

API design

One front door. Every way a report can arrive (the app, a dispatcher typing, an agent, a sensor on the truck) walks up to the same door and hands the record over the same way, because the only place you can catch a duplicate is the place where everything arrives. No side doors.

Two kinds of duplicates, two tools. An idempotency key handles the accidental kind: tap submit twice and both copies carry the same stamp, so the second one lands on the first. The matcher handles the human kind: the driver, the supervisor, and the phone call, all describing the same brakes. It compares the truck, the issue, and the clock. When it's sure, it merges. When it's close, it asks the dispatcher. Either way, one click puts it back.

The rest of the surface stays small. Read a request and its whole history. Move it through the lifecycle, where the door refuses illegal moves. And subscribe to changes instead of asking on a timer. That last one is the choice everything else leans on: a fleet of agents asking "anything new?" every few seconds is millions of calls that mostly hear nothing, while a fleet of agents that subscribed is silent until a truck actually needs something. The driver texts in build two are just the first subscriber. A shop hookup later is the same wire.

Every caller shows a permission slip at the door: what it may do, how often, and every knock gets logged.

Agent vs. human boundary

The agent does the work you can undo: turn a rambling phone report into a structured one, propose a merge, set a severity, escalate, send the text. A person does the rest: take a safety flag off, close out a brake job (the law already wants a qualified human signing that before the truck rolls), spend the money. The line isn't about which of them is smarter. It's about what you can take back.

When the agent is wrong, the costs are lopsided on purpose. A false flag costs a dispatcher one click. A missed brake report costs a tow truck on the shoulder. So it flags too eagerly, merges undo in one click, and if the merges start missing, it drops back to asking permission until it earns the automation again.

The one agent I wouldn't build yet is anything that calls shops and books work. That's real money moving on a record we haven't made trustworthy yet.

Success metrics

Targets set before the build, re-set once the Part 1 baselines come in.

  • Duplicate records per hundred real issues: down 90% or more, measured from merge events.
  • Status calls per dispatcher per day: down 60% or more, from call logs.
  • Median time-to-triage for safety-critical reports: under 15 minutes.

One guardrail, with the arithmetic. Forty requests per dispatcher per day, one in ten truly safety-critical, flagging at twice that rate to be safe: call it eight confirmations a day, about two minutes of work, inside a ten-tap budget. Both of those inputs are assumptions, and I say that as someone who likes his own arithmetic a little too much - the baseline data replaces them first. Past the budget we retune, because an alert everyone ignores is worse than no alert.
